\begin{abstract} Visual question answering (VQA) systems for blind and low-vision (BLV) users must do more than return an answer: they should recognize when an answer is unreliable, identify a likely visual failure mode, and suggest a corrective retake. This article presents an offline reliability study of a lightweight layer around frozen VQA models that combines image-level visual-sufficiency triage, human-labeled image-quality defect diagnosis, selective-prediction diagnostics, and defect-conditioned retake guidance. Using VizWiz-VQA and VizWiz-QualityIssues, real answerability labels are joined with seven human-annotated quality defects: blur, bright, dark, obstruction, framing, rotation, and unrecognizable content. Small prediction heads trained on frozen CLIP, DINOv2, and MobileNetV3 image embeddings estimate visual sufficiency and defects, while frozen ViLT and BLIP VQA models provide answers and confidence scores. The strongest triage head reaches AUROC 0.7926 with CLIP; the strongest defect head reaches mAP 0.6309 with DINOv2. To evaluate guidance we introduce two offline proxies: the Guidance--Defect Match Rate (GDMR), which measures whether the suggested retake action targets an annotated defect, and the Answerable-Image Retake Burden (AIRB), which measures how often retake requests would be issued for answerable images. Ungated, CLIP obtains GDMR 0.7196; when guidance is issued only after confidence-based refusal at a 90\% target coverage, CLIP reaches gated GDMR 0.875 at gated AIRB 0.050 under the ViLT gate. Selective-prediction value is model-specific for the two answerers evaluated: auxiliary signals do not significantly improve the ViLT confidence ranking after false-discovery-rate correction, but they do improve the weaker BLIP sequence-probability ranking, by up to 0.0179 AURC. Within this offline protocol, defect diagnosis is best viewed not as a replacement for confidence-based risk ranking, but as an interpretable refusal-and-guidance mechanism whose ranking value depends on the underlying VQA model; validating the guidance with BLV users remains essential future work. \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{V}{isual} question answering (VQA) is increasingly framed as an assistive interface: a blind or low-vision user captures an image, asks a spoken question, and expects a task-relevant answer. VizWiz made this promise concrete by collecting images and questions from blind users in everyday settings, and in doing so exposed a central obstacle for deployment: the visual evidence is often incomplete, poorly framed, blurred, dark, bright, rotated, or simply insufficient to answer the question at hand \cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. Subsequent work around the VizWiz ecosystem has reinforced that this difficulty is structural rather than incidental. Answer grounding remains hard in images taken by people with visual impairments, salient objects in those images differ systematically from those in conventional benchmarks, assistive captioning systems still struggle to transfer from curated data, and even newer egocentric video-based benchmarks remain challenging despite richer visual context \cite{chen2022grounding,dognin2020captioning,reynolds2023salient,song2024videoqa}. In other words, the practical problem in assistive VQA is not only to answer correctly when the image is good enough, but also to recognize when the image--question pair is likely to yield an unreliable answer, which is precisely the setting our reliability layer is designed for.

A substantial literature now studies this reliability problem through selective prediction and abstention. Reliable VQA work showed that a system should sometimes prefer saying ``I do not know'' to returning a confident error, and that learned selection functions can improve the risk--coverage trade-off over naive confidence heuristics \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving}. More recent work extends this agenda to broader vision-language reasoning, including selectively answering visual questions, identifying unreliable black-box vision-language outputs through consistency and uncertainty cues, and reducing unnecessary abstention once a model has become too conservative \cite{eisenschlos2024selectively,khan2024consistency,srinivasan2024selective}. That literature is essential because it clarifies how to rank answer risk, but it usually stops at the decision to answer or abstain. For assistive use, however, refusal alone is not enough: a user also needs a reason for the failure and guidance on how to recover, which is the part of the problem our paper brings into the foreground.

A parallel thread of work studies image quality in assistive imagery more directly. VizWiz-QualityIssues showed that image-quality assessment for blind-photographer images must move beyond abstract perceptual scores and instead identify concrete downstream-relevant defects, such as blur, poor lighting, obstruction, framing problems, and unrecognizable content \cite{chiu2020assessing}. User-centered studies tell a similar story from the interaction side: blind users actively try to diagnose recognition failures by changing viewpoint, distance, background, and framing, yet error recovery remains effortful and unreliable without explicit support from the system itself \cite{hong2024blinderrors}. At the same time, prior assistive-vision work has typically isolated one piece of the loop---answerability, quality prediction, grounding, captioning, or user behavior---rather than integrating when to refuse, why to refuse, and how to guide a re-shot inside one reliability pipeline. Our contribution is to connect these pieces by treating defect diagnosis not as an auxiliary benchmark alone, but as an explanatory and recovery-oriented layer for assistive VQA.

A further challenge is that not all confidence signals mean the same thing. In discriminative models, maximum-softmax confidence is easy to extract and, while often miscalibrated, has a well-established post-hoc calibration literature \cite{guo2017calibration}. In autoregressive generation, by contrast, raw sequence probability is a less stable proxy for correctness: recent work shows that sequence likelihood can mis-rank candidate quality and often benefits from explicit recalibration or context-sensitive reweighting before it can serve as a dependable confidence measure \cite{zhao2022slic,lin2024csl}. This distinction matters directly for assistive VQA because modern systems increasingly mix discriminative and generative answerers whose confidence semantics are not interchangeable. Our paper therefore asks a narrower and more deployment-relevant question than whether auxiliary signals ``help'' in the abstract: when does answerability and defect information actually improve selective prediction beyond the base VQA confidence, and when is its main value instead to explain refusals and guide recovery?

We answer this question by building a lightweight reliability layer around frozen VQA models rather than proposing a new answerer. The layer predicts visual sufficiency and image-quality defects from frozen visual embeddings, harvests VQA confidence from a frozen answerer, evaluates selective-prediction behavior, and maps predicted defects to retake guidance. One scoping choice should be stated up front: the triage and defect heads studied here operate on image embeddings only. VizWiz answerability is defined for an image--question pair, so an image-only head estimates whether the image provides sufficient visual evidence in general, not whether it answers the specific question asked; we therefore describe the triage signal as image-level visual sufficiency throughout, and treat question-conditioned triage as a required extension rather than a solved component. This design lets us separate risk ranking from user-facing diagnosis, and it yields four contributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We join VizWiz-VQA answerability labels with VizWiz-QualityIssues defect labels to study visual-sufficiency triage, defect diagnosis, refusal, and retake guidance inside one assistive VQA pipeline.
    \item We evaluate lightweight triage and multi-label defect heads on frozen \clip{}, \dinov{}, and \mobilenet{} embeddings, keeping the reliability layer modular and computationally lightweight.
    \item We introduce two offline guidance proxies with fully explicit denominators: the Guidance--Defect Match Rate (\arr{}), which scores agreement between the suggested retake action and the annotated defects, and the Answerable-Image Retake Burden (\frr{}), which scores how often retake requests would fall on answerable images. We report both ungated diagnostics and refusal-gated rates, and we are explicit that these are offline proxies for potential actionability, not measured user outcomes.
    \item We show that selective-prediction gains from auxiliary signals are model-specific for the two answerers evaluated: they do not significantly improve the \vilt{} confidence ranking after correction, but they do improve the weaker \blip{} sequence-probability ranking, while defect diagnosis remains valuable in both settings as an interpretable refusal-and-guidance mechanism.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Assistive Visual Question Answering and the VizWiz Lineage}
Assistive visual question answering is motivated by a real accessibility setting rather than a closed benchmark game. VizWiz established that distinction by collecting images and spoken questions from blind users, thereby surfacing three properties that are far less prominent in curated VQA benchmarks: conversational questions, poor image capture conditions, and a substantial prevalence of unanswerable examples \cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. More recent accessibility-oriented work has extended this line of inquiry in two complementary directions. Context-VQA argues that users who cannot see an image often need answers that are conditioned not only on image content but also on the surrounding use context, such as whether an image appears in shopping, travel, or news settings \cite{naik2023contextvqa}. VizWiz-LF then shows that blind and low-vision users often benefit from richer answers containing explanation and suggestion, while also documenting that generated long-form answers are especially vulnerable to hallucinating details when the image is blurry, irrelevant, or otherwise unanswerable \cite{huh2024longform}. Taken together, these studies show that assistive VQA requires systems that can recognize failure modes in realistic user images rather than merely maximize answer accuracy, which is precisely the setting in which our work introduces a separate reliability layer around a frozen VQA answerer.

\subsection{Image Quality Assessment for Real-World Assistive Images}
A second line of work studies why images captured in the wild fail downstream vision tasks. VizWiz-QualityIssues is central here because it links images taken by blind users to human judgments about recognizability and concrete defect categories such as blur, framing, brightness, darkness, obstruction, and rotation \cite{chiu2020assessing}. In the broader no-reference image quality assessment literature, recent methods increasingly emphasize authentic, in-the-wild degradations rather than synthetic distortions, reflecting the fact that real deployment images contain mixed technical and semantic failures that are harder to score with a single scalar label \cite{sun2021blindquality}. Work on visually impaired user-generated content pushes this further: Mandal \emph{et al.} build a large perceptual dataset of low-quality images captured by visually impaired users and propose automated feedback aimed at helping users mitigate common technical distortions when retaking photographs \cite{mandal2023helping}. Our paper builds on this trajectory but shifts the unit of analysis from generic visual quality to task-facing defect diagnosis for assistive VQA, using human-labeled defect signals not only to predict quality problems but also to explain refusals and drive recovery guidance.

\subsection{Selective Prediction and Abstention in VQA}
Selective prediction provides the formal language for saying ``answer when reliable, abstain when risky'' and is therefore the natural starting point for reliable assistive VQA \cite{elyaniv2010foundations}. In VQA specifically, Whitehead \emph{et al.} showed that naive confidence thresholding can be too brittle and that learned selection mechanisms can improve the coverage--risk trade-off, while Dancette \emph{et al.} demonstrated that stronger selectors can be learned by borrowing information from peer models \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving}. More recent work extends selective answering to contemporary vision-language models: Srinivasan \emph{et al.} study how to reduce needless abstention in vision-language reasoning, and Khan and Fu analyze black-box uncertainty and consistency signals for detecting unreliable vision-language model (VLM) responses \cite{srinivasan2024selective,khan2024consistency}. This literature mainly treats abstention as a ranking problem over answer risk; our work is complementary in asking when auxiliary answerability and defect signals improve that ranking, and in separating the ranking function from the user-facing need to explain a refusal and recommend a corrective retake action.

\subsection{Confidence Calibration and Uncertainty in Vision-Language Models}
Confidence quality is a crucial variable in any selective-prediction pipeline, because auxiliary signals should only help when the base confidence signal leaves meaningful headroom. For discriminative classifiers, modern calibration work has made temperature scaling the standard post-hoc reference point for repairing overconfident softmax outputs without changing class order \cite{guo2017calibration}. Autoregressive generation is more complicated. In neural machine translation, Wang \emph{et al.} show that severe miscalibration can persist at inference time even when training-time behavior appears better behaved \cite{wang2020inferencecalibration}. In natural-language generation more broadly, Lin \emph{et al.} argue that raw generated-sequence likelihood is a noisy confidence surrogate because it conflates semantic correctness with syntactic and phrasing effects, and they show that reweighting sequence likelihood can substantially improve reliability as a correctness signal \cite{lin2024csl}. Recent multimodal studies report a similar pattern at the vision-language level, finding persistent miscalibration and a tendency for multimodal large language models to answer rather than admit uncertainty \cite{chen2024unveiling}. We therefore position our \vilt{}--\blip{} comparison not as a universal claim that generative confidence is always inferior to discriminative softmax, but as a targeted empirical test of whether auxiliary defect and triage signals become more valuable when the underlying confidence channel is weaker or less well aligned with correctness.

\subsection{Actionable Feedback and Camera Guidance for Blind Users}
The final gap concerns actionability. Many assistive systems can produce an answer, a caption, or a confidence score, yet far fewer tell the user how to recover from a failed capture. Mandal \emph{et al.} come closest by proposing automated feedback to help visually impaired users take better photographs, but their emphasis is primarily on mitigating technical distortion rather than on tying those signals to question answerability and refusal decisions \cite{mandal2023helping}. Conversely, long-form assistive VQA work shows that explanations and suggestions are desirable in principle, while also demonstrating how easily generative systems can hallucinate when the underlying image is not dependable \cite{huh2024longform}. What remains largely missing is an evaluation of whether a refusal is accompanied by guidance that at least targets a genuine capture problem, or merely increases user burden. Our contribution targets that gap with an offline evaluation: we turn predicted defect categories into deterministic retake guidance and score the result with the guidance-agreement proxies $\arr$ and $\frr$, including refusal-gated variants that restrict guidance to the refused subset. Whether such guidance actually helps a blind user capture a better image is a question these proxies cannot answer; it requires the user study we outline as future work.

\section{Data Construction and Split Protocol}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source Datasets}
We build the study around two complementary VizWiz resources. VizWiz-VQA provides real images captured by blind users together with spoken questions, ten human answers, and an answerability flag indicating whether the question can be answered from the image \cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. VizWiz-QualityIssues provides human annotations for image-quality defects in the same assistive-photography setting, including blur, bright, dark, obstruction, framing, rotation, and unrecognizable content \cite{chiu2020assessing}. Using these two resources together lets us study not only whether a system should answer, but also whether it can identify an annotated visual defect that may explain the failure when it should not.

\subsection{Joining Labels and Enforcing the Frozen-Knob Protocol}
The two datasets are joined by image filename and split, and we retain only examples that have both VQA answerability labels and quality-issue annotations. This produces 24,319 rows in total, comprising 20,000 training rows and 4,319 validation rows. We further divide the validation portion into a calibration subset of 1,295 rows and a report subset of 3,024 rows. This split is central to the paper's evaluation discipline: all operating thresholds, temperature-scaling parameters, and learned selective-prediction risk models are fit only on the calibration subset, and all final model-performance comparisons and statistical tests are computed on the held-out report subset (descriptive statistics of the full joined dataset, such as label prevalence and whole-dataset answer accuracy, are reported as such and labeled explicitly). This frozen-knob rule prevents threshold tuning on the same examples used for final reporting.

\subsection{Label Definition and Class Imbalance}
For quality defects, we follow the VizWiz-QualityIssues annotation protocol: each image is labeled by five crowdworkers, and a defect is treated as present when at least two annotators select it \cite{chiu2020assessing}. After joining, the answerable rate is 0.7214. The defect labels are substantially imbalanced. Framing and blur are common, with positive rates 0.5606 and 0.4161, respectively, while bright, dark, and obstruction are much rarer, with positive rates 0.0539, 0.0563, and 0.0352. These skews matter for evaluation: they make aggregate accuracy misleading, motivate per-defect AUROC and AUPRC reporting, and make recovery analysis more informative than a single pooled score.

An image may still be answerable despite containing a real defect, and conversely an unanswerable image may fail for reasons that are only partially captured by any one defect label. This joined dataset therefore supports the paper's central decomposition into answerability triage, defect diagnosis, refusal, and recovery rather than collapsing all failures into one binary notion of correctness.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\subsection{Answerability and Defect Prediction}
Because the joined VizWiz labels are substantially imbalanced, we separate threshold-free ranking metrics from thresholded operating-point metrics. For answerability triage, we report AUROC and AUPRC to measure how well the score orders answerable and unanswerable examples across all thresholds. We then report F1 and balanced accuracy at the calibration-selected operating point. This combination is deliberate: AUPRC is especially informative when positives and negatives are skewed, whereas balanced accuracy prevents the majority answerability class from dominating the summary \cite{saito2015prroc}. We also keep a majority-class baseline to show whether a learned triage head improves on a trivial deployment rule. This metric bundle directly supports our contribution by evaluating, separately and honestly, whether the reliability layer can screen answerability under the class imbalance that characterizes assistive imagery.

For defect diagnosis, we treat the task as seven-label multi-label prediction and report per-defect AUROC and AUPRC, together with macro-F1, micro-F1, and mean average precision (mAP). Let $L=7$ denote the number of defect labels and $\mathrm{AP}_{\ell}$ the average precision for label $\ell$; then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{mAP} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \mathrm{AP}_{\ell}.
\end{equation}
Per-defect reporting matters because blur, framing, and unrecognizable content behave very differently from rare defects such as bright, dark, and obstruction, and aggregate scores can otherwise make a detector look more uniform than it really is. Reporting both macro- and micro-averaged summaries therefore lets us separate overall usefulness from minority-label behavior, which is exactly the distinction needed when defect labels are used not only for prediction but also for explanation and recovery.

\subsection{Selective Prediction}
We evaluate answer-versus-refuse behavior through the selective-prediction lens of risk versus coverage \cite{elyaniv2010foundations,geifman2017selective,geifman2019selectivenet}. After sorting report-set examples by decreasing trust score $s_{(1)} \geq s_{(2)} \geq \cdots \geq s_{(n)}$, where higher values mean ``more willing to answer,'' we define the empirical selective risk at retained prefix length $k$ as
\begin{equation}
R_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} \left(1-a_{(j)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $a_{(j)} \in [0,1]$ is the VizWiz VQA accuracy of the retained example. Coverage is $k/n$, and the area under the empirical risk--coverage curve is
\begin{equation}
\aurc = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} R_k.
\end{equation}
Lower $\aurc$ is better because it means the scoring rule pushes errors later in the answered queue and therefore leaves lower risk on higher-coverage retained sets \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving,eisenschlos2024selectively,khan2024consistency,srinivasan2024selective}. This is the right headline metric for our paper because the deployment decision we study is answer versus refuse, not calibration in isolation.

Calibration is still relevant, but we use it as an auxiliary diagnostic rather than a substitute for risk ranking. We compare raw global VQA confidence with temperature-scaled confidence \cite{guo2017calibration}, and when calibration behavior is discussed we report expected calibration error (ECE),
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|B_b|}{n}\left|\mathrm{acc}(B_b)-\mathrm{conf}(B_b)\right|,
\end{equation}
where $B_b$ is confidence bin $b$. We interpret ECE cautiously because its value depends on binning and related design choices, a point emphasized in later calibration studies \cite{nixon2019measuringcalibration,minderer2021revisiting}. One structural fact governs how these diagnostics are read: global temperature scaling with a single positive temperature is a strictly monotonic transformation, so it cannot change the confidence ordering and therefore cannot change $\aurc$. We consequently report temperature scaling only as a calibration diagnostic (via ECE and reliability diagrams), never as a risk-ranking competitor. Defect-conditioned calibration, by contrast, applies different temperatures to different examples and \emph{can} reorder them, so it participates in the $\aurc$ comparison. Against this background, we compare the following score families for each frozen VQA model: global confidence, defect-conditioned calibration, learned risk scores combining confidence with predicted defects, triage score alone, learned confidence-plus-triage scores, learned full-stack scores using confidence, triage, and predicted defects, and a confidence-plus-ground-truth-defects model used only as an oracle diagnostic of defect information. This comparison grid is what allows us to make the paper's central model-specific claim without assuming in advance that defect signals must beat confidence.

\subsection{Offline Guidance Proxies: \arr{} and \frr{}}
\label{sec:guidance_metrics}
Standard abstention metrics stop at the decision to answer or refuse, but assistive use requires a user-facing next action. We therefore evaluate retake guidance with two complementary offline proxies. We emphasize the word \emph{proxies}: no user retakes an image in this study and no second image is evaluated, so these measures score whether the suggested guidance \emph{targets an annotated defect}, not whether following it would recover the answer. Let $U$ be the set of unanswerable examples and $A$ the set of answerable examples on the evaluated split. For image $i$, let $\hat{d}_i$ be the top-scoring predicted defect if its probability exceeds a decision threshold ($\hat{d}_i=\varnothing$ otherwise), and let $D_i$ be the set of ground-truth defects. We define the Guidance--Defect Match Rate as
\begin{equation}
\arr = \frac{1}{|U|}\sum_{i \in U} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat{d}_i \in D_i\right],
\end{equation}
so $\arr$ measures whether the chosen retake recommendation corresponds to at least one annotated failure mode on unanswerable images; an example with $\hat{d}_i=\varnothing$ counts as a miss because no guidance is offered. We define the ungated Answerable-Image Retake Burden as
\begin{equation}
\frr = \frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{i \in A} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat{d}_i \neq \varnothing\right],
\end{equation}
which counts how often the system would ask for a retake on images that are, in principle, answerable. $\frr$ is deliberately a burden proxy rather than a ``false'' rate: an answerable image may contain a genuine defect, and an answerable image may still be answered incorrectly by the frozen model, in which case a retake could even be useful. A limitation shared by both proxies is that matching an annotated defect does not establish that the defect caused unanswerability: a blurred image may be unanswerable because the requested object is absent, in which case reducing blur would not recover the answer.

We also report refusal-gated variants at target coverages of 90\%, 80\%, and 70\%, where retake guidance is issued only when the VQA confidence falls below a calibration-selected refusal threshold. Because gating changes the population over which each rate is computed, we report the gated results with fully explicit denominators. Let $R$ denote the refused set and $G \subseteq R$ the refused examples that receive guidance ($\hat{d}_i \neq \varnothing$). We report four quantities:
\begin{align}
\text{cond.\ guidance precision} &= \frac{|\{i \in R \cap U : \hat{d}_i \in D_i\}|}{|\{i \in R \cap U : \hat{d}_i \neq \varnothing\}|},\\
\text{end-to-end match coverage} &= \frac{|\{i \in R \cap U : \hat{d}_i \in D_i\}|}{|U|},\\
\text{gated retake burden} &= \frac{|\{i \in R \cap A : \hat{d}_i \neq \varnothing\}|}{|A|},\\
\text{refusal precision} &= \frac{|R \cap U|}{|R|}.
\end{align}
The first quantity is conditional on the refused-unanswerable subset and can be high even when that subset is small; the second reports how much of the \emph{entire} unanswerable population actually receives matching guidance under the gate, which is the honest end-to-end number; the third and fourth characterize burden and refusal quality. Alongside each rate we report its raw numerator and denominator counts. The gated metrics therefore separate two different questions: ungated defect prediction answers the diagnostic question ``what is wrong with this image?'', whereas the gated quantities describe the refusal-gated offline policy ``issue guidance only after refusing.''

Finally, because framing alone affects a majority of images, a trivial policy could achieve a nontrivial match rate without learning anything. We therefore compare the learned defect head against reference policies with identical scoring: always predicting framing, predicting the most prevalent defect among unanswerable images, sampling defects by prevalence, predicting a defect uniformly at random, and an oracle that predicts a uniformly random ground-truth defect.

\subsection{Joint Versus Cascade}
To quantify architectural effects, we compare a cascade design with separate answerability and defect heads against a joint multi-task head, and we summarize the difference as the change in answerability AUROC between the two designs. This comparison is intentionally narrow: the goal is not to claim a new multi-task learning result, but to determine whether head coupling materially changes triage quality once the visual backbone is frozen. That framing keeps the architectural comparison subordinate to the paper's main question, namely whether a lightweight reliability layer can provide trustworthy refusal, explanation, and recovery without retraining the VQA model.

Across selective-prediction comparisons, uncertainty is estimated with paired bootstrap confidence intervals on the held-out report subset, following the standard resampling logic that avoids strong parametric assumptions for complex metrics \cite{efron1979bootstrap}. Because we evaluate families of closely related hypotheses on the same report split, we additionally apply Benjamini--Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction within each family rather than interpreting nominal $p$-values one by one \cite{benjamini1995fdr}. This conservative testing protocol is essential to our contribution because the paper's main claim is deliberately conditional: it is just as important to show where auxiliary signals do \emph{not} improve selective prediction as where they do.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the proposed reliability
layer. The central design choice is role separation. A frozen
visual encoder transforms each image into a reusable embedding,
and lightweight prediction heads operating on that embedding
estimate two auxiliary properties: whether the image provides
sufficient visual evidence to support answering (image-level
visual sufficiency) and which image-quality defects are likely to
be present. In parallel, a frozen visual question answering (VQA)
model produces an answer and an associated confidence score.
These signals support three related but distinct operations:
ranking answer risk, identifying an annotated visual defect that
may have contributed to failure, and recommending how the user
might recover.

The proposed system does not retrain the underlying VQA model
or claim to improve its answer-generation capability. Instead, it
places a lightweight reliability layer around a frozen answerer.
This modular construction allows us to test confidence,
answerability triage, predicted defects, and their learned
combinations without changing the base VQA model. It also
supports the paper's central distinction: VQA confidence may
rank answer risk, whereas defect diagnosis can explain a refusal
and guide a corrective retake.

\subsection{Frozen Visual Backbones}

We compare three frozen image encoders: \clip{} ViT-B/32
\cite{radford2021clip}, \dinov{} ViT-S/14 \cite{oquab2023dinov2},
and \mobilenet{}-Large \cite{howard2019mobilenetv3}. Each backbone
produces a fixed visual representation for an input image. The
resulting embeddings are cached once and reused by all
downstream heads, avoiding repeated feature extraction and
ensuring that the answerability and defect experiments operate
on identical representations.

The selected backbones represent different visual-learning
strategies. \clip{} provides a language-supervised visual
representation, \dinov{} provides a self-supervised transformer
representation, and \mobilenet{} provides a comparatively
lightweight convolutional representation. Because all encoders
remain frozen, observed differences reflect the information
available in their representations and the behavior of the
lightweight heads rather than end-to-end backbone fine-tuning.

\subsection{Image-Level Visual Sufficiency Triage}
\label{sec:triage_method}

For each backbone, a lightweight multilayer perceptron predicts
the VizWiz answerability label from the image embedding alone.
Because VizWiz answerability is defined for an image--question
pair, an image-only head cannot capture question-dependent
answerability: for one photograph of a medicine bottle, ``what
color is the bottle?'' may be answerable while ``what is the
dosage written in small text?'' is not. What the head estimates
is therefore image-level visual sufficiency---whether the image
provides enough usable visual evidence for typical questions---%
and we use that term throughout. The reported triage AUROC
should be read accordingly: it measures how much of VizWiz
answerability is predictable from the image alone, which is
substantial in this dataset because many unanswerable examples
fail for purely visual reasons, but it is not pair-level
answerability. Question-conditioned triage (question-only,
concatenation, and image--question fusion heads, with the
image-only head retained as an ablation) is the immediate
extension of this work. The target also does not directly
represent whether the frozen VQA model will produce the correct
answer: an answerable example may still be answered incorrectly,
while an image containing one or more quality defects may remain
answerable.

The heads are trained on the training split using five random
seeds. Decision thresholds are selected exclusively on the
calibration subset, after which the selected thresholds are frozen.
Final performance is measured on the held-out report subset and
reported as the mean and standard deviation across seeds. The
same triage scores are later evaluated both independently and as
auxiliary inputs to learned selective-prediction models.

\subsection{Defect Diagnosis}

The defect head is formulated as a multi-label classifier over
seven image-quality conditions: blur, bright, dark, obstruction,
framing, rotation, and unrecognizable content. A multi-label
formulation is required because a single assistive photograph can
contain several simultaneous defects. For example, an image can
be both blurred and poorly framed, or both dark and partially
obstructed. Each defect is therefore assigned an independent
prediction probability.

The defect head has two primary user-facing roles. First, it
provides a possible explanation when the system refuses to
answer. Second, the predicted defect is mapped to a corresponding
retake instruction. The same continuous defect probabilities are
also evaluated as possible auxiliary features for selective
prediction. However, whether they improve risk ranking is treated
as an empirical question rather than assumed by the architecture.

\subsection{Frozen VQA Answerers and Confidence Harvesting}

We use two frozen VQA answerers with different output and
confidence structures. The first is \vilt{} \cite{kim2021vilt},
which produces a predicted answer through a discriminative answer
classifier. Its confidence is the maximum probability in the
model's softmax answer distribution. The second is
\blip{}-VQA-base \cite{li2022blip}, which generates an answer
autoregressively. Its confidence is the length-normalized
sequence probability, calculated as the geometric mean of the
probabilities assigned to the generated tokens.

Using both answerers enables a controlled comparison between a
discriminative maximum-softmax score and a generative
sequence-probability score. Neither answerer is fine-tuned during
the present study. Their predictions and confidence values are
harvested as fixed outputs so that the analysis isolates the
contribution of the surrounding reliability layer.

VizWiz answer accuracy is computed as

\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Acc}(y,\mathcal{A})
    =
    \min\left(\frac{m}{3},1\right),
    \label{eq:vizwiz_accuracy}
\end{equation}

where \(m\) is the number of exact matches between the model
prediction \(y\) and the ten human reference answers
\(\mathcal{A}\). This consensus score supplies the correctness
target used in the selective-prediction analysis.

\subsection{Selective-Prediction Feature Construction}

The VQA model's native confidence serves as the baseline
risk-ranking signal. We then examine whether auxiliary
reliability signals provide useful information beyond that
confidence. The candidate auxiliary inputs are the answerability
score produced by the triage head and the seven continuous
probabilities produced by the defect head.

For each VQA answerer, the evaluated alternatives include native
global confidence, temperature-scaled confidence,
defect-conditioned calibration, confidence combined with
predicted defects, triage score alone, confidence combined with
triage, and a full-stack model combining confidence, triage, and
predicted-defect probabilities. We additionally evaluate
confidence combined with ground-truth defects as an oracle
diagnostic. The oracle is not a deployable method; it tests
whether perfect binary defect labels would provide an inherently
better ordering of answer risk.

All temperatures, refusal thresholds, and learned risk models are
fit exclusively on the calibration subset. They are then frozen
and applied without modification to the report subset. This
frozen-knob protocol prevents the final risk--coverage results
from being influenced by post hoc tuning on the reported
examples.

\subsection{Guidance Mapping and Policy}

Each predicted defect maps to a fixed user-facing retake action,
listed exhaustively in Table~\ref{tab:defect_guidance}. The
mapping is deterministic so that guidance--defect agreement can
be traced directly to the accuracy of the predicted defect rather
than to the behavior of a separate language-generation model.

    \centering
    \caption{Deterministic defect-to-guidance mapping used by the

    \begin{tabular}{ll}

        \midrule

        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

The highest-scoring defect is selected when its probability
exceeds the decision threshold; when no defect exceeds the
threshold, the system issues no retake guidance, and on an
unanswerable example this counts as a guidance miss in \arr{}.
Only the single top-scoring defect is surfaced even when several
defects co-occur (for example, blur together with framing, or
simultaneous bright and dark annotations arising from mixed
lighting): a single concrete instruction avoids overloading an
audio interface, at the cost of ignoring compatible secondary
instructions. Whether the ``unrecognizable'' instruction is
specific enough to act on is questionable, and we flag it as the
least actionable mapping. On an unanswerable example, the
guidance is scored as a match when the selected defect is present
among the ground-truth defects. A retake suggestion issued for an
answerable example contributes to retake burden. These two
outcomes are measured using \arr{} and \frr{}, respectively.

The refusal-gated policy does not issue guidance whenever the
defect head detects a quality issue. Instead, the confidence gate
first decides whether the VQA answer should be accepted or
refused. Defect-based guidance is presented only after refusal.
This ordering is important because many answerable images still
contain genuine quality defects; unconditional defect guidance
would therefore create unnecessary retake requests even when the
available visual evidence is sufficient to answer the question.

\subsection{Selective-Decision and Recovery Workflows}

Figure~\ref{fig:decision_recovery_workflows} provides a more
detailed view of the selective-prediction and recovery procedures.
The left panel follows an example from the frozen VQA model to
the final decision. The model produces both an answer and a
confidence score. Calibration-subset data are used to fit
temperature parameters, refusal thresholds, and learned risk
models. These choices are frozen before the report subset is
ranked by native confidence or learned risk. The resulting
ranking supports risk--coverage analysis and determines whether
an example is answered or refused.

Accepted examples contribute to the answered subset and its
associated risk. Refused examples are passed to the predicted
defect head, which selects a likely failure mode and maps it to
retake guidance. Recovery is therefore downstream of refusal:
the defect head does not independently veto an answer that has
already passed the confidence gate.

The right panel distinguishes the two recovery policies used in
the evaluation. Under the ungated diagnostic policy, predicted
defects are applied independently of the answer/refuse decision.
This setting measures the intrinsic capacity of the defect head
through \arr{} on unanswerable examples and the potential
diagnostic burden through \frr{} on answerable examples. Under
the refusal-gated policy, guidance is issued only for examples
rejected by the confidence gate. Gated \arr{} and gated \frr{}
therefore characterize recovery and burden within the subset
that would actually receive retake instructions during
deployment.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

\subsubsection{Backbones and Preprocessing}
\clip{} ViT-B/32 is loaded through \texttt{open\_clip} with the
original OpenAI weights and its native preprocessing pipeline,
producing 512-dimensional image embeddings. \dinov{} ViT-S/14 is
loaded from the official \texttt{facebookresearch/dinov2} hub
release (\texttt{dinov2\_vits14}); images are resized to 256
pixels (bicubic), center-cropped to $224\times224$, and
normalized with ImageNet statistics, producing 384-dimensional
embeddings. \mobilenet{}-Large uses the torchvision
\texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V2} weights and their bundled transforms,
with the 960-dimensional penultimate feature used as the
embedding. All embeddings are extracted once, stored as
float16, and shared by every downstream head.

\subsubsection{Heads and Optimization}
The triage and defect heads are two-layer perceptrons: a single
hidden layer of width 256 with GELU activation and dropout 0.3,
followed by a linear output layer (1 logit for triage, 7 for
defects). The joint multi-task head shares this 256-unit trunk
and attaches two linear branches; its loss is the sum of the
triage and defect losses with defect weight 1.0, so the joint
and cascade configurations are approximately parameter-matched
apart from the shared trunk. All heads are trained with
binary cross-entropy with per-class positive weighting
$w_{\ell} = n^{-}_{\ell}/n^{+}_{\ell}$ computed on the training
split, using AdamW (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay
$10^{-4}$), batch size 256, at most 50 epochs, and early stopping
on calibration-split AUROC with patience 7. Training uses
mixed-precision autocast on GPU. Each head is trained with five
seeds $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$; reported head metrics are the mean and
standard deviation of the per-seed report-split metrics.
Decision thresholds are selected on the calibration split by
maximizing F1 over a 99-point grid on $[0.01,0.99]$ (per label
for the defect head). The guidance policy in the reported
experiments uses a fixed 0.5 probability cutoff for issuing
guidance; a calibration-selected per-label alternative is
evaluated in the threshold-sensitivity analysis.

\subsubsection{Frozen Answerers and Correctness Target}
\vilt{} is \texttt{dandelin/vilt-b32-finetuned-vqa}; its
confidence is the maximum softmax probability over the fixed
answer vocabulary, and questions are truncated to 40 tokens.
\blip{} is \texttt{Salesforce/blip-vqa-base} decoded greedily
with at most 10 new tokens; its confidence is the
length-normalized sequence probability
\begin{equation}
c = \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_t\Big),
\end{equation}
the geometric mean of the per-token probabilities $p_t$ of the
$T$ generated non-padding tokens. Answer correctness uses the
VizWiz consensus rule of \eqref{eq:vizwiz_accuracy} with
lowercased, whitespace-stripped exact string matching. We note
explicitly that this is a simplified matcher: the official VizWiz
evaluation additionally normalizes punctuation, digits, articles,
and contractions, so our accuracy values can differ slightly from
official-script values, particularly for the generative answerer.
All selectors are compared under the same matcher, so
selective-prediction comparisons are internally consistent.

\subsubsection{Calibration, Risk Models, and Statistics}
Temperature scaling fits a single scalar by minimizing
negative log-likelihood with L-BFGS (at most 50 iterations) on
the calibration split. Defect-conditioned calibration fits one
temperature per predicted-defect group, falling back to $T=1$
for groups with fewer than 10 calibration examples. ECE uses 15
equal-width confidence bins. Learned risk models are logistic
regressions on standardized features (scikit-learn, LBFGS
solver, up to 2000 iterations) fit on the calibration split with
binarized correctness as the target. Confidence intervals use
2000 unstratified bootstrap resamples (seed 42). Paired
comparisons resample both scores on identical indices; the
two-sided $p$-value is twice the smaller tail fraction of the
bootstrap delta distribution. Benjamini--Hochberg correction is
applied within each answerer's family of selective-prediction
tests. The calibration subset is a 30\% carve-out of the joined
validation split (seed 42), and a runtime guard prevents any
threshold- or parameter-fitting function from receiving
report-split data. Experiments ran on Google Colab GPUs
(A100/L4/T4) with batch sizes auto-scaled per tier; the full
pipeline completes in a few GPU-hours, dominated by embedding
extraction and confidence harvesting.

\subsection{Protocol Summary}

The complete methodology follows a consistent frozen-knob
protocol. The visual backbones and VQA answerers remain frozen.
Answerability and defect heads are trained only on the training
split. Thresholds, temperatures, and learned risk models are
selected only on the calibration subset. All final measurements
are produced on the held-out report subset. Results are
aggregated across five random seeds where applicable, and the
evaluation uses bootstrap confidence intervals, paired-bootstrap
comparisons, and Benjamini--Hochberg false-discovery-rate
correction for the relevant test families.

These choices define the scope of the contribution. The proposed
system is not a new end-to-end VQA model. It is an attachable
reliability layer that decomposes the assistive interaction into
answerability triage, defect diagnosis, selective prediction,
refusal explanation, and recovery. The methodology tests the
contribution of each signal separately before measuring their
learned combinations, allowing the value of auxiliary features
to be reported as model-dependent rather than assumed to be
universal.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Data Sanity}
Taken together with the reliability-layer schematic in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} and the joined-label structure in Figure~\ref{fig:label_distribution}, the report-split evaluation starts from a deliberately difficult operating point rather than from a tuned end-to-end VQA system. The joined dataset contains 24{,}319 rows, with 20{,}000 training examples and 4{,}319 validation examples (1{,}295 calibration and 3{,}024 report rows). As whole-dataset descriptive statistics, the frozen \vilt{} model obtains mean VizWiz answer accuracy 0.1853 (mean confidence 0.4469) over all 24{,}319 rows and the frozen \blip{} model obtains 0.1707 (mean confidence 0.3669); on the 3{,}024-row report split used for all selective-prediction results, \vilt{} reaches 0.2540 and \blip{} reaches 0.2106. The gap between the full-dataset and report-split accuracies is expected rather than anomalous: the full-dataset figure is dominated by the 20{,}000 training-split rows, and the training and validation portions of VizWiz differ in answerable proportion and question mix; a per-subset breakdown (accuracy and answerable rate for the training, calibration, and report subsets) accompanies the released results manifest.

Both frozen answerers leave substantial reliability headroom, which is exactly the setting we target: not building a stronger answerer, but quantifying when to trust, when to refuse, and how to guide recovery around a fixed one. Their low absolute accuracy also bounds the practical meaning of every operating point reported below, a limitation we return to in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Visual-Sufficiency Triage}
Figure~\ref{fig:backbone_comparison} and Table~\ref{tab:core_results} show that image-level triage is useful but not sufficient on its own. \clip{} performs best, reaching AUROC $0.7926 \pm 0.0005$ and AUPRC $0.8811$. \dinov{} reaches AUROC $0.7769 \pm 0.0005$, while \mobilenet{} reaches AUROC $0.7512 \pm 0.0009$. Because these heads see the image only, the reported AUROC measures how much VizWiz answerability is predictable from visual sufficiency alone (Section~\ref{sec:triage_method}); disentangling the visual, linguistic, and interaction components of that score is exactly what the question-conditioned comparison is designed to do. All three backbones exceed the majority-class F1 baseline of 0.809, with model F1 in the range 0.823--0.836, but the more conservative balanced-accuracy range of 0.59--0.66 shows that triage remains imperfect under class imbalance. Recent reliable-VQA work has shown that abstention quality depends not only on whether a signal correlates with error, but on how finely it orders risk across examples \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving}. Our result is consistent with that distinction: triage captures answerability structure, but it is not the same thing as a universally strong risk-ranking signal.

The guidance results show why diagnosis should be evaluated separately from risk ranking. \clip{} obtains the highest ungated guidance--defect match rate, $\arr$ 0.7196, followed by \dinov{} at 0.6907 and \mobilenet{} at 0.6278. Because framing alone is annotated in 56\% of images, trivial policies are a mandatory reference here: an always-framing policy and prevalence-based policies can achieve substantial match rates without learning any image-specific signal, and the learned head must be read against those baselines rather than against zero.

The corresponding ungated retake burdens are high, near 0.78. Across all 22 \vilt{} selective-prediction tests in this family, no positive improvement over global confidence remains significant at FDR 0.05. This behavior is consistent with prior reliable-VQA findings that good abstention depends on the quality of the base correctness score, and it is also compatible with work showing that discriminative posteriors can remain reasonably calibratable even when raw confidence is imperfect \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving,khan2024consistency,srinivasan2024selective,guo2017calibration,desai2020calibration}. Our contribution here is the honest negative result: auxiliary visual signals did \emph{not} beat a stronger discriminative confidence once correction for multiple testing was enforced.

The \blip{} robustness analysis changes the interpretation without changing the standard of evidence. \blip{}-VQA-base has report accuracy 0.2106 and a weaker global-confidence \emph{ranking}, with $\aurc=0.6242$; we phrase this carefully, because a worse AURC establishes a weaker correctness-ranking signal on the evaluated report split, not necessarily worse calibration, which is a different property. In this setting, auxiliary reliability signals do help. With \clip{} defects, a confidence-plus-predicted-defects model improves $\aurc$ by $+0.0153$, and the full stack improves $\aurc$ by $+0.0179$; both survive BH-FDR correction. With \mobilenet{} defects, the corresponding improvements are $+0.0137$ and $+0.0172$, also significant after correction. Confidence-plus-triage improves \blip{} risk ranking for all three backbones, from $+0.0046$ to $+0.0069$. \dinov{} full-stack reaches $+0.0090$ with $p=0.043$, but does not survive FDR; triage alone remains much worse than confidence; and the ground-truth-defect oracle does not help \blip{}, with $-0.0007$ and $p=0.82$. The most careful interpretation is therefore not that ``defects help selective prediction'' in general, but that learned continuous auxiliary signals can improve a weaker base confidence signal. That reading is plausible in light of newer evidence that autoregressive or sequence-level confidence can be poorly calibrated at inference, that raw generation likelihoods can be weak proxies for answer quality, and that token-level or self-evaluation alternatives can sometimes correlate better with correctness \cite{wang2020inferencecalibration,ren2023selfevaluation,wang2024myanswerisc,groot2024overconfidence}. Our contribution is to show that this generative-confidence weakness manifests concretely in assistive VQA, but only for the weaker model and only under corrected testing.

Figures~\ref{fig:calibration_risk} and \ref{fig:selective_diagnostic}, together with Tables~\ref{tab:selective} and \ref{tab:blip_selective}, make the role separation visually explicit. For \vilt{}, Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_risk} shows that temperature scaling does not materially alter the risk-coverage ordering, and Figure~\ref{fig:selective_diagnostic} shows that positive bars for auxiliary models are too small or too unstable to survive correction. For \blip{}, the same diagnostic figure shows larger and corrected gains for predicted-defect and full-stack models, while the oracle result stays null. That oracle null constrains the interpretation: whatever the learned predicted scores contribute, it is not the binary defect taxonomy itself. We resist the stronger reading that the predicted scores necessarily encode a ``richer description of defects.'' Continuous predicted scores are functions of the backbone embedding, so their gain may equally reflect embedding-derived visual difficulty, label uncertainty, class prevalence, or correlations with answerability inherited from training. Distinguishing these explanations requires controls---risk models built on thresholded predicted labels, on backbone embeddings without defect supervision, and on heads trained against permuted defect targets with identical capacity---which we specify in the released pipeline and leave to the revised analysis.

What the present evidence does establish is a separation of roles: explanatory value, guidance value, and ranking value need not rise and fall together.

\subsection{Qualitative Refusal Behavior}
Figure~\ref{fig:qualitative} is useful because it shows why the paper should not be read as only a metric-improvement exercise. The grid is rule-sampled from the report split using fixed seed 7 rather than hand-picked, and its rows separate high-confidence correct answers, high-confidence wrong answers, good refusals with matching defect-based retake guidance, and wrong-reason refusals. The second row matters most from an assistive perspective: visually fragile examples can still elicit confident but wrong answers, which is exactly the failure mode that motivates refusal. The bottom row shows the remaining limitation just as clearly: the system can refuse honestly yet still offer the wrong visual reason. Recent work on VQA reliability and multimodal uncertainty likewise emphasizes that identifying uncertainty is not the same as explaining it \cite{whitehead2022reliable,khan2024consistency,groot2024overconfidence}. Our contribution is to place those two requirements in the same evaluation loop by measuring both whether the system should refuse and whether its guidance targets an annotated defect.

\subsection{Joint Versus Cascade}
Finally, the architectural comparison shows that some design choices matter less than others. The joint head gives small observed AUROC gains over the cascade design: $+0.0013$ for \clip{}, $+0.0028$ for \dinov{}, and $+0.0037$ for \mobilenet{}. We have not established these differences with paired confidence intervals, so we treat them as small observed gains rather than confirmed effects; the joint and cascade heads are approximately parameter-matched (Section~\ref{sec:implementation}) with unit multi-task loss weights. The pattern suggests that backbone choice and base-confidence quality matter more than whether triage and defect prediction are coupled in a single head. That synthesis matches the rest of the section: backbone quality affects triage and diagnosis, base VQA confidence determines whether auxiliary signals help ranking, and defect heads remain valuable for explanation and recovery even when their ranking gains are small. This is the practical conclusion our Results section is designed to support.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Recent work on reliable VQA has shown that abstention can substantially improve system trustworthiness, but most of that literature evaluates success primarily through risk--coverage tradeoffs and selective-answering performance \cite{whitehead2022reliable,dancette2023improving,eisenschlos2024selectively,khan2024consistency,srinivasan2024selective}. In assistive VQA, that framing is necessary but incomplete, because many failures originate not only in the answerer but also in the image capture process itself: VizWiz questions come from blind users, and a substantial fraction of images are genuinely hard to interpret because of blur, poor framing, occlusion, exposure problems, or unrecognizable content \cite{gurari2018vizwiz,chiu2020assessing}. Our results therefore support a sharper separation of roles than a pure abstention formulation suggests: confidence is the primary candidate for ranking answer risk, while predicted defects contribute most reliably by explaining refusals and grounding recovery.

Why this separation holds strongly for \vilt{} but less strongly for \blip{} is consistent with a broader calibration literature. In discriminative classification, posterior-style confidence is often imperfect yet still reasonably amenable to post-hoc calibration, and temperature scaling remains a strong baseline for converting logits into better-behaved confidence estimates \cite{guo2017calibration,desai2020calibration}. By contrast, confidence derived from autoregressive generation is often harder to interpret at the answer level: sequence probabilities are affected by tokenization, end-of-sequence behavior, decoding dynamics, and length normalization, and encoder--decoder models have been shown to exhibit substantial calibration problems even when token probabilities look locally sensible \cite{kumar2019calibration}. More broadly, several studies in language generation argue that raw sequence likelihood is an incomplete proxy for answer correctness because semantically equivalent responses can receive different probabilities, motivating answer-level self-evaluation or semantic uncertainty measures instead of relying on token probability alone \cite{kadavath2022language,kuhn2023semantic}. Our \vilt{}/\blip{} comparison fits this pattern: auxiliary triage and defect signals are not universally helpful, but they become useful when the base confidence signal is weaker as a risk ranker.

The \blip{} results also clarify why predicted defects can help while the ground-truth-defect oracle does not. Recent multimodal reliability work increasingly favors richer auxiliary signals---for example, consistency under perturbation, semantic agreement across responses, or explicitly calibrated verbal confidence---rather than assuming that a single symbolic attribute will linearly order risk \cite{khan2024consistency,zhang2024vluncertainty,xuan2025seeing}. That perspective aligns with our finding that the gain for \blip{} comes from learned interactions with continuous predicted signals, not from defect labels as an oracle ordering. In other words, predicted defects appear to matter less as discrete declarations of ``the'' failure mode and more as soft descriptors of visual difficulty that can be fused with a weaker confidence signal. Our contribution is to demonstrate this distinction in an assistive VQA setting with human-labeled real-world defects: learned predicted signals can improve selective prediction for a weaker answerer, even when ground-truth defect labels by themselves do not.

The guidance results make the practical implication more concrete. VizWiz images can remain answerable despite containing genuine quality defects \cite{gurari2018vizwiz,chiu2020assessing}, so an ungated diagnosis module naturally produces a high retake burden if it is allowed to recommend retakes on every example. That is why refusal-gated guidance is the right operating view for this setting: retake guidance should be issued only after the system has already decided that answering would be unsafe, so that diagnosis supports abstention instead of competing with it. Under that design, the point of defect prediction is not to replace confidence, but to attach an interpretable, potentially actionable reason to each refusal. Our contribution is therefore not merely a better abstention score, but a modular offline evaluation of a reliability layer that separates ranking, defect identification, and guidance, and shows that the best balance among them remains model-dependent within the two answerers studied.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The most consequential design limitation is that the triage and
defect heads are conditioned on the image alone. VizWiz
answerability is a property of the image--question pair, so the
reported triage AUROC measures image-level visual sufficiency
rather than pair-level answerability (Section~\ref{sec:triage_method}),
and the reported score cannot separate what is attributable to
visual defects, to question language, or to their interaction.
The question-conditioned comparison specified in the released
pipeline---image-only linear probe and MLP, question-only head,
concatenation, image--question fusion, and models that add VQA
confidence---is required before the triage component can be
described as answerability prediction.

This study is further limited to the examples obtained by joining the
VizWiz-VQA and VizWiz-QualityIssues train and validation splits.
The hidden VizWiz test answers are not available for evaluation,
and the join retains only images for which both answerability and
quality annotations are present. Consequently, the reported
results characterize the shared, labeled portion of VizWiz rather
than the complete VizWiz distribution, other assistive-image
datasets, or images collected through a deployed application.
Moreover, the calibration and report subsets originate from the
same joined validation distribution. Although their separation
prevents thresholds and risk models from being fitted on the
reported examples, it does not test whether confidence,
calibration, or refusal thresholds remain reliable under changes
in devices, users, environments, or question distributions.
Predictive uncertainty and post-hoc calibration can degrade under
distribution shift \cite{ovadia2019uncertainty}; therefore, the
present contribution should be interpreted as controlled
within-dataset evidence for the proposed reliability layer rather
than as a guarantee of deployment-time calibration.

The frozen VQA answerers are restricted to \vilt{} and
\blip{}-VQA-base, and both obtain low absolute accuracy on the
joined data. The observed contrast between discriminative
maximum-softmax confidence and generative length-normalized
sequence confidence is therefore evidence of model-dependence
within these two answerers, not proof that all discriminative and
generative architectures will behave similarly.
Accessibility-oriented VQA also differs materially from
conventional machine-understanding benchmarks in its
questions, image conditions, and user goals \cite{cao2022different}. Recent
evaluations focused on blind and low-vision (BLV) users further
show that modern generative models can produce plausible but
incorrect visual details, particularly when the submitted image
is unanswerable \cite{huh2024longform}.
Repeating the complete frozen-knob
protocol with stronger contemporary vision-language models,
alternative decoding procedures, and additional confidence
estimators is therefore necessary before the model-dependent
finding can be generalized beyond the two answerers evaluated
here.

The answer-correctness target also inherits limitations from the
standard VizWiz evaluation rule. Accuracy is computed from exact
matches against the ten human reference answers using the
VizWiz consensus score. This protocol provides a consistent basis
for comparing all selectors in the present experiments, but it can
penalize a generated answer that is semantically appropriate yet
lexically different from the references. This concern is especially
relevant for open-ended generative VQA, for which recent work has
identified the inflexibility of exact-match evaluation and proposed
semantic alternatives \cite{ji2024flexible}. The risk labels used in
our selective-prediction analysis should therefore be understood
as correctness under the adopted VizWiz metric rather than as a
complete measurement of usefulness, factual adequacy, or
linguistic equivalence. Our contribution remains a controlled
comparison because every risk-ranking method is evaluated using
the same correctness definition, but future work should test
whether the model-dependent conclusions persist under validated
semantic and human evaluation.

The selective-prediction analysis examines confidence,
temperature scaling, defect-conditioned calibration,
answerability-triage scores, predicted-defect features, their
learned combinations, and a ground-truth-defect oracle.
Other signals---including answer consistency across stochastic
decodes, representation-space uncertainty, question-conditioned
features, retrieval evidence, or explicit self-evaluation---could
produce different risk rankings. Five-seed reporting, paired
bootstrap comparisons, and BH-FDR correction reduce sensitivity
to initialization, sampling variation, and multiple testing within
the specified experimental families, but they do not establish
that the observed ordering is invariant across datasets, VQA
models, prompts, decoding strategies, or calibration populations.
Accordingly, the study supports the narrower conclusion that the
tested auxiliary signals are model-dependent under the specified
protocol, rather than the broader claim that confidence must
always dominate or that defect signals can never improve
selective prediction.

The proposed \arr{} and \frr{} metrics evaluate whether a
predicted defect corresponds to a predefined corrective action,
but the deterministic defect-to-action mapping has not been
validated with blind or low-vision users. A label-level recovery
hit does not establish that the resulting instruction is clear,
safe, contextually appropriate, or sufficiently precise to help the
user capture a better image. Likewise, \frr{} measures the
frequency with which an answerable example would trigger a
retake suggestion; it does not directly measure frustration,
physical effort, time, repeated capture attempts, or abandonment.
The refusal-gated results provide a more policy-relevant
burden estimate than ungated diagnosis, but a longitudinal user
study is still required to measure whether guidance improves the
next image and whether users consider the intervention worthwhile.
The rule-sampled qualitative grid avoids deliberate cherry-picking,
yet it remains illustrative and cannot substitute for evaluation
with the intended users. Thus, \arr{} and \frr{} should be viewed
as reproducible offline proxies introduced by this work, not as
complete measures of accessible user experience.

Finally, the seven quality labels explain important image-capture
failures but do not cover every reason a VQA answer may be
incorrect or unsafe. Errors can arise from ambiguous questions,
optical-character-recognition failures, fine-grained recognition,
small visual evidence, unsupported inference, missing world
knowledge, or mismatch between the question and the visible
content. The current system predicts image-level defect labels and
does not identify the spatial region responsible for the answer or
the refusal. Prior work on VizWiz answer grounding shows that
localizing relevant evidence remains difficult, particularly when
the evidence is small or the question requires text recognition
\cite{chen2022grounding}. Spatial grounding, region-specific
camera instructions, question-aware diagnosis, and evaluation of
successive retake attempts therefore remain future work. The
present contribution is intentionally narrower: it establishes a
lightweight image-level layer for deciding when to trust, identifying
likely capture defects, and evaluating guidance--defect agreement without
claiming to resolve every source of VQA error.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

We have introduced a lightweight reliability layer for assistive VQA that leverages real answerability and image-quality labels from the VizWiz dataset to study when a blind user's visual question should be answered or declined, and which annotated capture defect may explain a declination. Small prediction heads on frozen visual encoders (\clip{}, \dinov{}, \mobilenet{}) can diagnose image defects (blur, lighting, framing, and related conditions) and estimate image-level visual sufficiency, supporting deterministic retake instructions. In our modular offline evaluation, these predictions achieve high guidance--defect agreement when used judiciously: for example, gating retake advice on a 90\% coverage refusal threshold yields \clip{}-gated \arr{} 0.875 at \frr{} 0.050 under the \vilt{} gate, with the conditional-denominator caveats detailed in Section~\ref{sec:guidance_metrics}.

At the same time, our selective-prediction analysis reveals that the utility of auxiliary signals depends on the VQA model, at least within the two answerers evaluated. For the discriminative \vilt{} model (which chooses from a fixed vocabulary), max-softmax confidence is already well-aligned with correctness on this data, so defect and triage features provide no significant AURC gain after BH-FDR adjustment. By contrast, the generative \blip{} model's length-normalized sequence probability is a weaker correctness-ranking signal on the evaluated report split, and its risk ranking is significantly improved by learned combinations of confidence, triage, and predicted defects. In other words, augmenting confidence with auxiliary cues mattered primarily when the base ranking signal was weak. This observation is consistent with recent findings in VQA reliability: open-ended generation tends toward overconfidence relative to closed-set classification, and as Whitehead et al. showed, naively thresholding a model's softmax can limit coverage severely \cite{whitehead2022reliable}.

The practical lesson is thus to treat defect diagnosis as an interpretable refusal companion rather than a blanket substitute for confidence-based ranking. Start with the VQA model's confidence as the primary reliability signal, and only introduce learned auxiliary selectors when held-out evaluation indicates that the base ranking is weak. When an answer is refused, the defect head can then give the user a likely visual reason and a concrete retake suggestion. This design neither overstates the power of defect labels nor discards them. The immediate next steps are those the analysis itself demands: question-conditioned triage, trivial-policy guidance baselines, threshold sensitivity, and evaluation with stronger contemporary vision-language answerers---followed by the study this offline protocol cannot replace, a retake study with blind and low-vision users measuring whether the guidance actually improves the next capture.

\section*{Ethics and Data Statement}
This study uses only the publicly released VizWiz-VQA and VizWiz-QualityIssues datasets and inherits their consent and collection terms; no new data were collected from users, and no interaction with blind or low-vision participants took place. Images taken by blind photographers can inadvertently contain sensitive visible content, including documents, screens, medication labels, and barcodes. We therefore restrict qualitative displays to a rule-sampled grid, reviewed for inadvertent disclosure of personally identifying information before publication, and we do not redistribute the images themselves. The defect-to-guidance mapping is a research artifact evaluated offline; it has not been validated with the intended users, and the reported proxies must not be interpreted as evidence of real-world benefit or safety for BLV users. Any deployment would additionally require attention to on-device privacy, since the images involved are captured in users' homes and daily lives.
